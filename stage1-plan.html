Input Representation

Stage 1 · now
Adopted

Each sample is the sequence of messages heard from one sender pseudonym, with copies of the same message (same messageID) heard by different receivers removed. Its length is T = 64 messages. Beacons arrive at 1 Hz in VeReMi-Extension, so T = 64 is about 64 seconds of driving and keeps 79% of vehicles; T = 128 is run as a sensitivity check. Every sample is cut to exactly T messages, with no padding, and an equal number of windows is drawn per vehicle, so sequence length cannot reveal the class.

Every timestep carries a 13-dimensional feature vector built from BSM fields:

  • Position (4): sender-claimed position (pos_x, pos_y), and the receiver's own GPS position at rcvTime (from its type-2 record).
  • Velocity (4): sender-claimed velocity vector and the receiver's own velocity vector.
  • Acceleration (2): sender-claimed acl_x, acl_y.
  • Relative geometry (2): range and bearing from the receiver to the claimed position.
  • Timing (1): log of the inter-message gap Δτ = rcvTime(t) − rcvTime(t−1).

Comparing what a vehicle claims with where the receiver actually is gives the key signal for position falsification. The claimed position is a median 81 m from the receiver for benign senders and 743 m for DoSRandom attackers.

Δτ catches timing anomalies. Benign beacons arrive about every 1 s with occasional missed messages, while GridSybil sends bursts (15% of gaps under 0.5 s).

Time of day is not used: it alone separates the 07–09 h and 14–16 h scenario groups and would invalidate the transfer test. The true sender ID, the *_noise fields and ground-truth positions are never inputs.

Train, validation and test splits are grouped by (time window, physical vehicle) across all four attack scenarios, so no vehicle appears in more than one split.

Professor's original

Each trajectory is a multivariate time series of length T (a hyperparameter chosen from the VeReMi Extension trajectory length distribution). Every timestep carries a 13 dimensional feature vector built from BSM fields: Position (4): sender reported (posx, posy) and receiver observed (pos_n x, pos_n y); Velocity (4): sender and receiver speed in vector form; Acceleration (2): aclx, acly; Time (3): sin and cos of time of day (cyclic encoding), plus the inter message gap Δτ = rcvTime(t) minus rcvTime(t−1). Including both sender and receiver views matters because the mismatch between what a vehicle claims and what the receiver observes is the key signal for position falsification and replay attacks. The Δτ feature catches timing anomalies, since BSMs normally arrive every 100 ms.

Architecture

Stage 1 · now
Adopted
  • Encoder: TimesNet with four stacked TimesBlocks (inner width d_ff = 64, 3 Inception kernel sizes, top-k = 3 periods) and no time-of-day embedding.
  • Periods: chosen per sample from FFT frequencies f ≥ 2.
  • Outputs: per-timestep states H (T × d), and an embedding z (dimension d ∈ {128, 256, 512}) from global average pooling of H over time.
  • How it works: TimesNet uses FFT to find dominant periods and reshapes the sequence into 2-D, so convolutions capture both short- and long-range temporal patterns.
  • Comparison: a Transformer encoder with the same inputs, outputs and pretraining is trained as an ablation, to measure whether TimesNet's period modelling helps on mostly aperiodic vehicle motion.

Stage 1: Self-Supervised Pretraining (no attack labels)

Stage 1 · now
Adopted

Three objectives are trained jointly, using training-split vehicles only. The reconstruction decoder reads H; all other heads attach to z. All extra heads are discarded after pretraining.

Masked reconstruction

  • Each sample uses one of two masking strategies with equal probability: random masking (exactly 25% of timesteps) or block masking (one contiguous block of round(r·T) steps, r ~ U(0.20, 0.30), i.e. 13–19 steps at T = 64).
  • Masked steps are replaced by a learned mask token.
  • A two-layer MLP decoder reconstructs the masked steps from H, trained with mean squared error over masked steps. The reconstruction targets are sender position (relative to the window), velocity, acceleration and log-Δτ.
  • The model must beat a linear-interpolation baseline on block masks.

Physical constraint heads (P1–P3)

Three binary classifiers (MLPs, BCE loss). With probability 0.5, one physics violation is injected into the window, and each head predicts whether its violation was injected:

  • P1, kinematic violation: a speed spike or position jump at a random step.
  • P2, speed consistency violation: the claimed speed is scaled without moving the positions to match.
  • P3, heading feasibility violation: an impossible sharp turn of heading or path at one step.

Because the labels come from our own random injection, they never encode the attack label and need no map. The original rules computed on real data are reported only as diagnostics: on real windows, H1 fired only on attackers, H2 found no violations above 70 km/h, and H3 flagged 33% of benign windows.

Contrastive learning (SimCLR style, InfoNCE loss)

Two augmented views of each sample form a positive pair, using physically consistent augmentations:

  • a common shift of 3–5 m applied to both the sender and the receiver positions;
  • a time stretch s ∈ [0.95, 1.05] (velocity × s, acceleration × s², Δτ ÷ s);
  • a random 80–100% temporal crop taken by subsampling steps, without interpolation.

Augmentations must not imitate attack artifacts. Per-step GPS jitter and speed scaling on their own are not used.

Negatives are the other samples in the batch (one window per pseudonym per batch), plus hard negatives from the same 50 m grid cell and same scenario group, from a different pseudonym at least 10 minutes apart, down-weighted by β = 0.5. Settings: temperature τ = 0.1, batch size 256, and a projection head d → d → 128 that is discarded afterward.

Joint loss

  • L = λ1·L_rec + λ2·L_InfoNCE + λ3·L_P1 + λ4·L_P2 + λ5·L_P3, with each loss normalised to a comparable scale.
  • λ1 = 1, λ3 = λ4 = λ5 = 0.3, λ2 ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 1}, plus one run with automatic uncertainty weighting.
  • Checkpoints are chosen without labels (held-out reconstruction error and embedding quality on a pretraining validation slice).

Memory Bank and Anomaly Scoring

Stage 2 · deferred
Adopted

After fine-tuning, we freeze the encoder.

  • Building the bank: pass the training-split trajectories through it, without using their labels, and store the L2-normalised embeddings as the memory bank.
  • Scoring: for a new trajectory, retrieve its K nearest embeddings with FAISS (cosine similarity), K ∈ {5, 10, 20}. The anomaly score is the mean cosine similarity to those K neighbours.
  • Threshold: a score below θ means anomalous. K and θ are chosen on the validation split, and the test split is used once.
  • Deployment: an anomalous vehicle's update would be excluded from a federated-learning round. That is the motivating deployment; FL itself is not simulated.
Professor's original

What changed and why

One line each. Click an item for the full reasoning and measured numbers in the plan review.

  1. 1 Hz, not 100 ms: the measured median gap is 1.000 s.
  2. Receiver "observed" position = receiver's own GPS: VeReMi has no receiver-side measurement of the sender.
  3. Time of day removed, range and bearing added: time of day perfectly separates the two scenario groups (shortcut). The vector stays 13-dimensional.
  4. T = 64 with fixed-length, length-matched samples: attackers send longer histories, so length would leak the class.
  5. New vehicle-grouped split: 89.7% of benign test vehicles currently have a near-copy in train.
  6. TimesNet details fixed: d_ff = 64 keeps it at 2.3–9.2M parameters instead of 37.5–600M; per-sample periods; no time embedding.
  7. Block length = ratio × T: a 20–40-step block doesn't fit the 20–30% mask ratio at T = 64.
  8. The decoder reads H, not z: reconstructing individual timesteps needs the per-step states.
  9. Physics heads detect injected violations: the original rules leaked the label (H1), were empty (H2) or caught simulator quirks (H3). The heads are renamed P1–P3 to avoid clashing with the thesis hypotheses.
  10. Augmentations made physically consistent: jitter and speed scaling resembled attack artifacts. The timestamp offset is dropped because time of day is no longer a feature.
  11. Road-segment negatives become grid-cell negatives within the same group: there is no map, and "other time of day" means the other scenario group.
  12. Small λ sweep and label-free checkpoint selection instead of a full grid: a full grid is 243 configs × 3 seeds, and choosing by accuracy uses labels.
  13. Stage 2 uses the attack codes we have: A1–A9 are not in our data, and data replay is A17, not A16.
  14. Memory bank built from unlabeled training data: choosing only "legitimate" trajectories needs labels. FL is treated as the deployment motivation only.

A Sybil attacker invents fake cars

Input pipeline as built (preprocessing and feature engineering)

What the encoder actually reads (status 2026-10-07): every received message kept, 13 features per message, links cropped to at most 64 messages, every window padded to 64 rows with a mask (one fixed shape, 64 × 13). Benign + GridSybil is the first trial of the whole pipeline; all attack types come later (DataReplay / DoS links are 1–2 messages, so they need a per-receiver time window). Outputs go to src/data/ (never under data/).

Pipeline

Solid boxes are data on disk, dashed boxes are notebooks. The window-size notebook is read-only: it only chooses T.

data on disk notebook model
  • 13 features per message: claimed position, velocity and acceleration (x, y), receiver position and velocity (x, y), range, bearing (one wrapped angle), log Δτ. Never inputs: time of day, true sender id, noise fields, ground truth.
  • Link = what one receiving car heard from one sender pseudonym, in time order. Every received copy is kept.
  • Normalisation: mean and std from train real rows only; padding rows are 0, a missing first log Δτ is 0 (the train mean).
  • T = 64 kept (compute is not an issue): it cuts 5.1% of links, against 24.6% at T = 24 (22,230 vs 148,131 cut points; 26% vs 63% of messages sit in a cut link). T = 24 stays an ablation.

Crop, pad to 64 and mask

Each link is cut into consecutive, non-overlapping pieces of at most 64 messages. Every piece is padded at the end to 64 rows, so the encoder always sees T = 64. The mask marks real rows (1) and padding (0).

Why fixed 64: one shape, one model config and standard batches. The cost, accepted by the user: 71.0% of rows are padding, about 2.7× the padding rows of the bucketed variant (26.3%), so the mask is essential. A fixed T without padding keeps only 21% of messages at T = 64; crop + pad uses every message exactly once. Alternative, one setting away: length buckets {8, 16, 32, 64} (each piece padded only to the smallest bucket that fits, variable T per batch) by setting buckets = (8, 16, 32, 64) in src/pipeline/benign_gridsybil/encoder_input_T64.ipynb (current: buckets = (64,)). Rejected: resampling (invents motion, breaks Δτ), backfill (looks like a frozen-position attack), packing several links in one row (TimesNet mixes them).

  • Done: z = masked mean of H over real rows only; every block output is multiplied by the mask.

The pipeline

Each car logs where it is and every message it hears. We merge the two so every received message also carries the listener's own position, then line messages up per sender.

What one message looks like from the receiver's seat

Car R hears car S. The message says where S claims to be (dashed red marker). We measure that claim against where R itself is: dx, dy, the straight-line range, the bearing θ, and the relative velocity.

An attacker's claimed position is often far from any plausible spot: median claimed-to-receiver distance is 743 m for DoSRandom vs 81 m for benign senders (p99 1,540 m vs 384 m).

What to notice: the two recording windows (07:00–09:00 and 14:00–16:00) never overlap on this feature, so a model could tell which scenario a sample came from by reading the clock instead of the motion. That would make cross-scenario results meaningless, so we drop sin/cos time of day and use the two freed slots for range and bearing (decision D3).

3 · How far is the claimed position from the listener?
Distance between the sender's claimed position and the receiving car (metres): dot = median (p50), bar end = p99

What to notice: comparing what a sender claims with where the listener actually is carries real signal. DoSRandom claims positions far away (median 743 m), while GridSybil and DataReplay sit inside the normal benign range, so those two are the hard cases.

What was built (S1.2.1–S1.2.3)

  • TimesNet: 4 TimesBlocks, d = 128, d_ff = 64, 3 Inception kernels, top-3 periods per window. 2,301,312 parameters, within 0.06% of the planned 2.30M (finding F9).
  • Embedding: a 1-D convolution over the 13 features with zero padding (not circular, so the end of a window never leaks into its start) plus a sinusoidal position code. No time of day.
  • Periods from real rows only: each window's FFT ignores its padding. On a synthetic test this found the true period 10/10 times, against 9/10 when the padding was left in. Windows with fewer than 4 real rows fall back to period 1 (24% of period choices).
  • The mask everywhere: every block output is multiplied by the mask; z is the mean of H over real rows only. Output: H (B, 64, 128) and z (B, 128).
  • Untrained heads and loss definitions (preview of S1.3): a masker that hides exactly 25% of real rows, a 2-layer MLP reconstruction head, physics heads P1 speed jump / P2 speed scaled / P3 sharp turn (the injectors that create these violations are not built yet), a projection head (128, L2-normalised, NT-Xent τ = 0.1).

The notebook prints the untrained losses only as a demo. They mean nothing and are not results.

Decisions in this design
Periods picked per window
The reference code picks periods from the batch average, so a window's embedding would change with whichever other windows share its batch. Here each window gets its own periods (test: output unchanged by batch composition).
Frequencies start at f = 2
f = 1 means period = T, a one-row "grid" with nothing 2-D to compare. Starting at f = 2 guarantees at least two rows.
No time-of-day embedding D3
Time of day alone tells the 07–09 h group from the 14–16 h group (no overlap), which would break the 0709 ↔ 1416 transfer test.
Global average pooling, not flatten advisor's choice
The reference flattens all T × d values. Averaging gives one d-sized vector whatever T is and can skip padded steps. Recorded as a deliberate deviation from the reference.
Small bottleneck: d_ff = 64, 3 kernels F9
Keeps the model at 2.3–9.2M parameters instead of 37.5–600M (chart above).
Transformer kept as an ablation
Same inputs and the same (H, z) outputs (≈1.19M params at d = 128), so we can measure whether TimesNet's rhythm-finding actually helps on this data.
How TimesNet sees time — interactiveTimesBlock, one channel
ILLUSTRATIVE The signal below is synthetic, generated in your browser (not VeReMi data). It walks through what one TimesBlock does to one feature channel: find the strongest repeating periods, fold the series into a 2-D grid for each, then blend the results. One sample = 1 s (VeReMi beacons arrive at 1 Hz).
1The input: one feature over time

A window of T seconds of (made-up) speed; shaded bands mark the cycles of the period chosen in step 3.

2The spectrum: which rhythms are strongest?

A Fourier transform scores every frequency f (cycles per window); the 3 tallest bars become the periods p = T // f.

Bin 0 (the average level) is left out. Bin 1 is greyed: period = T gives a single row, not used. Each window picks its own periods (per-sample), not the batch average.
3The fold: 1-D becomes 2-D

The series is cut every p seconds and the pieces are stacked as rows.

Rows = successive cycles, columns = position within a cycle; a 2-D conv now sees both neighbouring seconds (along a row) and the same moment in the previous cycle (down a column).
4The blend: weight each period by its strength

Each of the 3 folded grids is processed separately, unfolded, then mixed with softmax(amplitude) weights.

output = w₁·branch(p₁) + w₂·branch(p₂) + w₃·branch(p₃) + input
Here the softmax uses the illustrative amplitudes from step 2. In the real model the amplitudes come from the learned embedding (averaged over its d channels), so how peaked the weights are is learned, not fixed.
Honest caveat. Vehicle kinematics are mostly aperiodic: pick "smooth cruise" and the spectrum has no clear peak, yet top-3 still picks three bins. Whether TimesNet's period trick helps on VeReMi is something the experiments will measure (and a null result will be reported as such).
