Part A · Step 2 · Sybil attacks & identities

A Sybil attack is one car pretending to be many

Every car broadcasts ~1 message per second (position, speed, heading), signed with a pseudonym (identity), a temporary ID. A Sybil attacker is one real car using several pseudonyms at once.

HarmFake traffic jams, outvoting honest cars, flooding the channel.
Hard to stopEach pseudonym has a valid certificate (the PKI gives every car several, for privacy). Only the content gives the attacker away.
Paper L140–152: “the vehicular PKI issues multiple pseudonym certificates to the same vehicle to ensure user’s privacy … one vehicles pretends to be simultaneously multiple entities on the road. This type of malicious behavior is called a Sybil attack.” Pseudonym letters are illustrative.
A Sybil attacker is one real car that broadcasts under several pseudonyms at once, so receivers see ghost cars. Certificates do not help, because the PKI legitimately gives each car several pseudonyms; only the content of the messages can expose it.

Part C · Step 8 · link, window & T

Cars broadcast, every receiver logs, and each log groups into links

1 · A broadcasts to everyone in range

1 · Broadcast~1 beacon (BSM) per second: position, speed, heading + pseudonym, to everyone in radio range (a few hundred metres).
2 · ReceiveEvery car in range hears it: one beacon, many receivers.
3 · LogsVeReMi keeps one log per receiver: everything it heard, plus its own GPS.
4 · LinksGroup one receiver’s log by sender pseudonym, in time order: a link = what car R heard from pseudonym P. No central server.
Labels: a car’s own broadcasts are all benign or all attack (an attacker’s messages before its attack starts are labelled genuine). As a receiver, a car holds links from benign and attacking senders mixed. Positions, ranges and pseudonyms in the animation are illustrative.
Every car broadcasts about once a second; everyone in range logs it. VeReMi keeps one log per receiver. Grouping one receiver's log by sender pseudonym in time order gives a link, our data unit.

Choice: T = 48 (≈ p90), because
1For a typical 11–14-message link T does not matter (same window, more padding). T only decides how the longest ~16% (> 32) are cut.
2Sequence signals (position change vs claimed velocity, ghosts drifting together) need tens of seconds; 48 keeps ~90% of tracks whole.
3TimesNet finds periods up to T/2: 24 s at 48 vs 16 s at 32. The planned block masking of 20–40 steps does not fit a 32-row window.
464 adds only +4 points of whole links for +8 points of padding and ~33% more compute. 32 and 64 stay as sensitivity checks (D1 said 64).

Part C · Step 12 · link, window & T

Smaller T cuts padding but loses context; length buckets avoid the trade-off

A long link is split, not lost, but patterns longer than T are: TimesNet sees periods up to T/2, 12 s at T = 24, 24 s at T = 48.

Links whole = links that fit in one window. Fixed T = 24: 75% fit whole; the rest are split.
Padding rows = empty rows (mask = 0) that fill a window up to T. Fixed T = 24: 42% of all rows.
Length bucket = each link goes into the smallest of 8 / 16 / 32 / 64 that fits; ~26% padding; batches are built per bucket.

Part C · Step 13 · link, window & T

Overlap and stride: how windows are cut from a link

Window = T messages taken from one link. Stride = how far the window moves before the next one is cut. Overlap = messages two neighbouring windows share = T − stride.
Stride = T (our data now): back to back, every message used once. Stride = T/2 = 24 at T = 48 (50% overlap): each window starts halfway through the previous one; every message except the ends sits in two windows.

Stride 48 · window 1 of 3 · messages 1–48

What overlap givesA pattern cut at a window boundary appears whole in the neighbouring window. Long links give more windows (a mild augmentation).
What it costs; why the gain is smallOnly links longer than T get extra windows (~9% at T = 48; most links are 11–14 messages). Near-duplicate windows of one link must not be contrastive negatives (D13 already masks pairs from the same pseudonym and run). Long tracks get more weight. One link’s windows stay in one split (the vehicle split ensures it). Overlap never mixes cars: a window holds one sender’s messages.
VerdictKeep no overlap (stride = T) as the default. Test stride 24 as an ablation; keep it only if it improves the check-set losses or later results.
Window counts: a link of n messages gives ceil(n / T) windows at stride T, and 1 + ceil((n − T) / 24) at stride 24 when n > T. Links longer than 48: src/eda/eda_window_size.ipynb (91% fit whole at T = 48). D13: agent.md §3.
Stride is how far the window moves; overlap is T minus stride. Today we cut windows back to back, so each message is used once; stride 24 would give long links extra, overlapping windows. Few links are longer than 48, so the gain is small: no overlap stays the default and stride 24 is an ablation.

Part D · Step 16 · Stage 1 (self-supervised pretraining)

After training: 48 × 13 numbers in, 128 numbers out

Like a face-ID codeNumbers in, numbers out; not a text model. Keep T = 48 at inference.
Long or short linksLong: several windows (average z). One message: 1 real row + 47 padding rows (mask = 0).
One pseudonym per windowIt never links two pseudonyms of one car. Many pseudonyms need a step on top: compare z of co-present pseudonyms, or a receiver time window.
Each window = one receiver + one pseudonym; the true sender is never an input. 13 features: D3 in agent.md §3 (bearing = one wrapped angle; no time of day). The grid shows only some of the 48 rows.
In: up to 48 messages by 13 features plus a mask. Out: z, 128 numbers that summarise how the track moves. It sees one pseudonym per window, so detecting many identities needs a step on top.
